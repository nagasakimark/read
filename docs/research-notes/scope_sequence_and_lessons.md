## 1. Principles for GPC ordering, and actual introduction orders in established programmes

### Takeaway
Every programme opens with a small set of high-utility single-letter consonants plus 1–2 short vowels so that VC/CVC words can be blended within the first week (s a t p i n in Letters and Sounds/Jolly/Little Wandle; m a s d t in RWI; a m s t in UFLI), then completes the alphabet before moving on. UK programmes (L&S, Little Wandle, Jolly, RWI) teach a "simple code" of ~one grapheme per phoneme — including vowel digraphs like ai/ee/igh/oa — in the first year, whereas UFLI (US) completes short vowels, consonant digraphs and blends first, and introduces VCe before vowel teams.

### Cited Findings
**Ordering principles (official guidance)**
- DfE: "Programme writers select which GPCs they are going to prioritise for teaching, as well as their order, so that the GPCs generate the most words at each stage of the programme." Most start with a simple code, "approximately one grapheme for each of the 44 or so phonemes (maybe including a few common alternative spellings such as 'c', 'k' and 'ck' for the sound /k/)", then a complex code "starting with the most common alternative graphemes." — [Reading Framework 2023, p.48](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- DfE: SSP programmes "focus on the most common [GPCs] that will generate the most words"; pupils then apply that to rarer GPCs. — [Reading Framework 2023](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- WWC: "Present consonants and short vowel sounds represented by single letters first, since these appear frequently in words... For example, the first group of phonemes taught could be /s/, /m/, /d/, /p/, /a/." Then "consonant blends (e.g., fl, sm, st) and common two-letter consonant digraphs (e.g., sh, th, ch)... Then teach long vowels with silent e, and finally two-letter vowel teams." "Start with the most common sound each letter represents, and introduce each letter sound one at a time." — [WWC 2016, Rec. 2, p.18](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf)
- WWC: blends should not be memorised as units — "teaching each sound in a blend and then asking students to blend the sounds together. A digraph makes a single sound and must be taught as a unit." — [WWC 2016, p.18](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf). DfE likewise: clusters "consist of separate phonemes and children should be taught to pronounce each one before blending them." — [Reading Framework 2023, p.45](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- WWC: start phoneme work with continuous consonants "(i.e., f, h, l, m, n, r, s, v, z)" because they are "easier for students to elongate." — [WWC 2016, Example 2.3](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf)

### Inferences
- Common opening set across programmes: s, a, t, p, i, n, m, d (all UK programmes have these in the first 8–10; UFLI has a, m, s, t, p, f, i, n). For a web app, an opening set like s a t p i n (then m d g o c k) is the best-documented UK option; UFLI's a m s t is the best-documented US option. Both put the first vowel in the first 1–2 items and add a second vowel by item ~5–8 to make CVC words possible immediately.
- Confusable letters get separated in practice: b is introduced well after d in all orders (L&S: d set 2, b set 5; RWI: d 4th, b 14th; UFLI: d 13, b 17; Jolly: d group 2, b group 3); e and i are separated by several weeks (L&S i set 2, e set 4; UFLI i 8, e 18). No primary document found that states the b/d separation principle explicitly — it is visible in the orders.
- Key UK vs US difference: UK programmes introduce vowel digraphs/trigraphs (ai, ee, igh, oa, oo, ar, or...) in Reception, *before* adjacent consonants (Phase 4) and split digraphs (Y1). UFLI does blends (CCVC/CVCC) within the alphabet stage, consonant digraphs, then VCe (lessons 54–62) *before* r-controlled and vowel teams (77–98), and adds syllable work and inflections (63–68) early.
- RWI puts split digraphs in Set 3 alongside vowel teams; L&S/Little Wandle put split digraphs in Year 1 (Phase 5); UFLI puts VCe immediately after consonant digraphs.

- Teach Your Monster to Read sequence not verified.

## 2. Word-structure progression

### Takeaway
All programmes progress VC/CVC → words with digraphs → adjacent consonants (CVCC/CCVC → CCVCC/CCCVC) → long vowels (split digraph and vowel teams) → r-controlled/diphthongs → alternative spellings/pronunciations → multisyllable/affixes, but the UK puts vowel digraphs before consonant clusters while UFLI puts clusters and VCe first.

### Cited Findings
- L&S Phase 4 explicit structure order: CVCC (lost), CCVC/CCV (stop, tree), CCVCC (trust), CCCVC (strap), CCCVCC (shrink). — [L&S summary](https://www.st-patricksrc.notts.sch.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2022/02/letters-and-sounds-progression-expectations.pdf)
- L&S Phase 3 sub-stages: 3.1 CVC words with letters/short vowels; 3.2 wider letters + some consonant digraphs + double letters; 3.3 less frequent consonant digraphs and some long vowel phonemes. — [same](https://www.st-patricksrc.notts.sch.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2022/02/letters-and-sounds-progression-expectations.pdf)
- DfE example cluster analysis: s-p-i-n (4 phonemes; cluster /s/ /p/), c-l-u-m-p (5), ch-o-m-p, s-p-l-a-sh, s-t-r-ee-t, th-r-ee, f-l-air. "Many children need extra support to blend words with consonant clusters, particularly when they occur at the start of words." — [Reading Framework 2023, p.45–46](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- DfE: spelling "goat" is harder than "dog" "because the sound /oe/ has different common spellings"; "Children learn to read more quickly than they learn to spell correctly... progress in reading must not be held back by whether or not they can spell accurately." — [Reading Framework 2023, p.46](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- WWC sound–spelling pattern taxonomy: consonant digraphs/trigraphs (th, sh, ch, ph, ng, tch, dge); blends (scr, st, cl, ft); silent-letter combos (kn, wr, gn, rh, mb); vowel teams (ea, oo, oa, igh, eigh); diphthongs (oi, ou); r-controlled (ar, er, ir, or, ur); long e (ee, ie, ea, e_e, ey, ei, y); long a (a_e, ai, ay, ei, ea, ey). — [WWC 2016, Table/Example p.25](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf)
- UFLI progression as listed in §1: CVC (1–34) → blends in longer-word review (35–41) → FLSZ/ck/digraphs (42–53) → VCe (54–62) → -es/-ed/-ing + closed/open syllables (63–68) → tch/dge/-le/y (69–76) → r-controlled (77–83) → vowel teams (84–94) → diphthongs (95–97) → silent letters (98) → affixes & spelling changes (99–110) → low-frequency spellings (111–118) → more affixes (119–128). — [UFLI S&S](https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/UFLI-Scope2.pdf)
- Little Wandle puts -s/-es endings in Reception Autumn 2 and -ing/-ed/-est + compound words in Reception Summer alongside adjacent consonants. — [Little Wandle reproduction](https://parc-eglos.croftymat.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/17/2023/03/Little-Wandle-for-Website.pdf)

### Inferences
- A web app can model words as phoneme/grapheme arrays and gate content by (a) GPCs known and (b) structure tier (VC, CVC, CVCC, CCVC, CCVCC, CCCVC, CCCVCC, then multisyllable), matching L&S Phase 4's explicit tiering.
- Because initial clusters are reported as harder than final clusters (DfE), CVCC can precede CCVC as in L&S.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found comparing UK "vowel digraphs early" vs US "blends/VCe first" orders on outcomes.

## 3. High-frequency and tricky/heart words

### Takeaway
Official UK guidance: keep exception words to a minimum early, teach them by noting the irregular part, and do not have pupils memorise high-frequency word lists because most HFWs are decodable once GPCs are known. WWC similarly recommends teaching both regular and irregular HFWs, but its examples include word walls and flashcards.

### Cited Findings
- DfE early exception-word example list: said, to, was, I, the, me, no, of, all, he, you, they, she, we, are, my, be, some, so, were, go. "Pupils are taught to read and spell these by noting the part that is an exception... in the word 'said', 's' and 'd' correspond to the phonemes /s/ and /d/ as usual, but 'ai' corresponds to the phoneme /e/." — [Reading Framework 2023, p.48](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- DfE: common exception words "contain GPCs that are unusual or have not yet been taught" (i.e., includes temporarily irregular). "Pupils should not be asked to learn lists of high frequency words. They can read most of these in the usual way... e.g. 'mum' and 'came'." — [Reading Framework 2023, p.48](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- WWC Rec. 3.5: "Teach regular and irregular high-frequency words so that students can recognize them efficiently"; 3.6: "Introduce non-decodable words that are essential to the meaning of the text as whole words." Example 3.8 lists irregular (e.g., from, said) vs regular (had, with) HFWs and suggests a word wall; Example 3.9: flashcard routine with 3–5 HFWs per student, adding words after three stars per card. For irregular words like "of" and "was" "simply tell students the words... and ask them to repeat the word." — [WWC 2016, pp. 28–30](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf)
- Programme counts/timing: Little Wandle — 3 tricky words in Reception Autumn 1, ~19 in Autumn 2, 9 in Spring 1, ~18 in Summer, ~16 in Y1 Autumn 2 etc. — [Little Wandle reproduction](https://parc-eglos.croftymat.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/17/2023/03/Little-Wandle-for-Website.pdf). Jolly Phonics — first tricky word "I" in the sh lesson; then 24 weekly units (Steps 2–3) totalling ~72 tricky words over Year 1+. — [Jolly map](https://henhamugley.essex.sch.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/Jolly-Phonics-progression-map-1.pdf)
- UFLI's 8-step lesson includes a dedicated "Irregular (heart) words" step. — [Spelling Test Buddy summary (third-party)](https://spellingtestbuddy.com/resources/ufli-scope-and-sequence/)
- Accent matters: Little Wandle notes put/pull/full/push/ask/half/father need not be tricky in some accents. — [Little Wandle reproduction](https://parc-eglos.croftymat.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/17/2023/03/Little-Wandle-for-Website.pdf)

### Inferences
- App design: maintain a per-word "irregular part" annotation (e.g., said → ai = /e/) and a "becomes decodable at GPC X" flag, so "tricky" words automatically convert to decodable once the GPC is taught (e.g., "he/she/we/me/be" once open-syllable e is taught; "my/by" once y=/igh/ is taught).
- WWC's word wall suggestion conflicts with the Reading Framework's caution about HFW lists and decorated walls (see §8); the DfE position is the more restrictive.

### Gaps
- Dolch (220 service words + 95 nouns) and Fry (1,000 instant words) lists were not retrieved from primary sources this session. UFLI heart-word list and "heart word" marking routine (mark the irregular part with a heart) not verified from UFLI primary documents. National Curriculum Y1/Y2 common exception word lists (English Appendix 1) not retrieved.

## 4. Advanced phonics: syllable types, division, schwa, morphology

### Takeaway
WWC and UFLI both teach syllable types (closed, open, VCe, vowel team, r-controlled, consonant-le) and division within K–2, plus inflections -s/-es/-ed/-ing, then prefixes/suffixes with spelling-change rules (doubling, drop-e, y→i) and some Latin/Greek elements (uni-, bi-, tri-; -tion/-sion). Division patterns are typically taught by frequency: VC/CV, then V/CV and VC/V, then V/V.

### Cited Findings
- WWC syllable-construction patterns with examples: closed (in-sect, stu-dent), VCe (com-pete, base-ball), open (pro-gram, tor-na-do), vowel team (train-er, neigh-bor-hood), vowel-r (char-ter, cir-cus), consonant-le (drib-ble, puz-zle). — [WWC 2016, p.25](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf)
- WWC: "Teach students about suffixes (e.g., -s, -ed, ...), forms of prefixes (e.g., dis-, mis-, pre-), and basic roots (e.g., aqua, cent, uni)"; e.g., add -ing to park, call, sing; strategy step "Circle recognizable word parts. Look for prefixes at the beginning, suffixes at the end." — [WWC 2016, pp.26–27](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf)
- UFLI morphology sequence: -s (20–21), -es/-ed/-ing (63–65), closed/open syllables (66–68), -le (75), -s/-es, -er/-est, -ly, -less/-ful, un-, pre-/re-, dis- (99–106), doubling/drop-e/y→i (107–110), -sion/-tion, -ture, -er/-or/-ist, -ish, -y, -ness, -ment, -able/-ible, uni-/bi-/tri- (119–127). — [UFLI S&S](https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/UFLI-Scope2.pdf)
- Division patterns (IMSE, Schukraft, 20 July 2020): VC/CV (cac/tus) "most common"; V/CV (lo/tus) more common VCV split; VC/V (rad/ish); V/V (me/te/or, vi/o/lin) least common. Teach VC/CV first, then V/CV and VC/V together, then V/V; when unsure, try both VCV divisions. IMSE lists 7 syllable types (closed, open, magic e, vowel team, bossy r, diphthong, consonant-le). — [IMSE Journal](https://imse.com/journal/article/syllable-division-decoding-strategies/)
- Schwa: "most commonly spoken vowel sound in English," occurs only in unaccented syllables, typically sounds like short u or short i; harder to encode than decode. — [IMSE Journal (2020)](https://imse.com/journal/article/syllable-division-decoding-strategies/)
- DfE: some programmes "continue to teach phonics for spelling, once pupils can read, including teaching further morphemes"; "Over the course of year 2 and into key stage 2" focus shifts; spelling taught systematically in Y2/KS2. — [Reading Framework 2023](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- DfE: pronunciation may need "tweaking" after sounding out (e.g., "mountain" — child may first say ai as long, then recognise the word) — a basis for "set for variability"/schwa correction. — [Reading Framework 2023, p.45](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)

### Inferences
- Six types (closed, open, VCe, vowel team, r-controlled, C-le) is the WWC set; some sources add diphthong as a separate 7th type (IMSE). For an app, syllable work can begin right after VCe (UFLI places open/closed syllables at lessons 66–68).
- Schwa is not a named lesson in the UFLI chart; programmes handle it implicitly (e.g., "a /ŏ/" in lesson 94, -le, -tion). Explicit schwa teaching in an app should come with multisyllable reading and include a "say it, then fix it to a real word" step.

### Gaps
- No primary Moats (*Speech to Print*) or Kilpatrick text retrieved; no source on Latin/Greek roots sequencing beyond K–2 (UFLI's uni/bi/tri; WWC's aqua/cent/uni).

## 5. Letter names vs sounds; uppercase

### Takeaway
UK synthetic phonics leads with sounds (some programmes delay names), but teaches that upper- and lower-case share name and sound; WWC introduces upper and lower case together and builds on children's known letter names. Research indicates letter names help children learn sounds (when the sound is in the name) and spell, while alphabet-only instruction shows little transfer to reading.

### Cited Findings
- DfE: "Programmes teach that each lower-case letter has a corresponding capital letter; they share the letter name and represent the same sound... both 'a' and 'A' are called /ae/ and are usually pronounced /a/. Some programmes teach the names of letters only once pupils have learnt to say the sounds." — [Reading Framework 2023, pp.48–49](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- RWI teaches sounds not letter names, pure sounds ("mmm" not "em"/"muh"). — [Oxford Owl](https://home.oxfordowl.co.uk/reading/reading-schemes-oxford-levels/read-write-inc-phonics-guide/)
- WWC: build on letters children know (e.g., letters in their name); "Introduce the letters in both uppercase and lowercase"; show a memorable picture of a familiar regular word with that phoneme (e.g., pig). — [WWC 2016, pp.18–19](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf)
- Piasta & Wagner (2010), *Reading Research Quarterly* 45(1), doi:10.1598/rrq.45.1.2: effects differed by alphabet outcome and instruction content; school-based > home-based; small-group > individual tutoring; "minimal evidence of transfer of alphabet instruction to early phonological, reading, or spelling skills." — [Abstract via Europe PMC / PMC2910925](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2910925)
- Treiman et al. (2008), *Cognition*, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2007.06.006: US children use letter names to learn sounds; better on letters whose sound is at the start of the name (v) than at the end (m) or absent (h); true across children with speech/language disorders. — [Europe PMC record](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2007.06.006)
- Treiman et al. (2020), *Psychological Science*, doi:10.1177/0956797619888837: 75 preschoolers spelled letter-name words (bead) more phonologically plausibly than controls (bed); "Words with letter names can help children move from prephonological spellings to spellings that symbolize at least some of the sounds." — [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797619888837)
- DfE citing Ehri et al. (2001): "PA instruction with letters produced larger effects on PA and reading than instruction without letters." — [Reading Framework 2023, fn 77](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)

### Inferences
- For Japanese ESL children who may already know letter names (from school English), the app could lead with sounds but explicitly connect names (Treiman shows names can scaffold sounds for letters like b, d, v, z; names are misleading for h, w, y, c, g).
- Uppercase: show both cases at introduction (WWC), but decodable text in early lessons is mostly lowercase; capitals needed for sentence starts and names.

### Gaps
- Piasta & Wagner effect sizes by outcome and findings on combined name+sound instruction were not extracted (full text blocked). No specific study on order of upper vs lower-case introduction retrieved.

## 6. Pacing

### Takeaway
Validated UK programmes move fast: L&S Phase 2's 19 GPCs in up to 6 weeks (~3–4/week), Jolly Phonics 1 sound/day (42 sounds in ~9 weeks), with the simple code finished in Reception and the complex code through Year 1 (end of Y1 phonics screening check); spelling/morphology continues in Y2. UFLI spans K–2 over 128 concept lessons.

### Cited Findings
- L&S timings: Phase 2 up to 6 weeks; Phase 3 up to 12 weeks; Phase 4 4–6 weeks (all Reception); Phase 5 throughout Year 1 (4 + 3 + 22 weeks). — [L&S summary](https://www.st-patricksrc.notts.sch.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2022/02/letters-and-sounds-progression-expectations.pdf)
- Jolly Phonics: "1 sound per day" in Reception; revise one group per week; Steps 2 and 3 are 12 weekly units each. — [Jolly map](https://henhamugley.essex.sch.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/Jolly-Phonics-progression-map-1.pdf)
- Little Wandle: Year 1 Summer 1 "Phonics screening check review – no new GPCs". — [Little Wandle reproduction](https://parc-eglos.croftymat.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/17/2023/03/Little-Wandle-for-Website.pdf)
- DfE: daily phonics from start of Reception; "Phonics sessions might be only ten minutes long in the first few days. However, by the end of Reception, children will need about an hour a day, maybe split into different sessions"; direct teaching daily "until they can decode". Case study: by end of Year 1 child "no longer needed the support of 'decodable' books." — [Reading Framework 2023, pp.50, 57, ~81](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- DfE: teachers should "make sure that children practise using the knowledge they have been taught in previous lessons until they can use it automatically"; keep-up support for those falling behind. — [Reading Framework 2023, p.51](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- UFLI: 148 lessons total; daily routine ~30 minutes. — [UFLI materials page](https://ufli.education.ufl.edu/foundations-materials/); [third-party summary](https://spellingtestbuddy.com/resources/ufli-scope-and-sequence/)

### Inferences
- ~4 new GPCs/week is consistent with L&S Phase 2 (19 in ≤6 wks incl. a starter week) and Little Wandle's Autumn 1 (~20 GPCs in a half-term). Jolly's 5/week is the fastest.
- For an app used by older ESL learners at lower daily dosage (e.g., 10–15 min/day), pace should be mastery-gated rather than calendar-gated.

### Gaps
- The specific claim "4 new GPCs per week" was NOT found in the July 2023 Reading Framework text (grep for "per week", "four new" returned nothing); it may be in the DfE SSP validation criteria (2021/2022), which I did not retrieve. Do not attribute it to the Reading Framework without checking.

## 7. Lesson structure

### Takeaway
Converging model: review → teach (explicit, small steps, modelled) → guided practice → independent practice/apply in decodable text and dictation → assess; UFLI's 8 steps, DfE's direct-teaching list and Rosenshine's 10 principles all align.

### Cited Findings
- DfE direct-teaching components for reading: "revise GPCs taught in earlier sessions; be taught new GPCs; practise reading words containing those GPCs; be taught how to read common exception words; practise reading 'decodable' phrases, sentences and books...; re-read these to build fluency." For writing: segment spoken words; choose letters for each sound; pencil grip; letter formation (no lead-in strokes); "respond to dictation... words in sentences that include only the GPCs and exception words they have learnt." "Dictation is a vital part of a phonics session." — [Reading Framework 2023, pp.51–52](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- DfE teacher behaviours: clear objectives; all children participate (e.g., "call and response"); "use activities that maximise the number of words children read and spell"; connect to previous learning; "demonstrate new learning in bite-sized chunks"; specific praise; assessment to identify those needing immediate support. — [Reading Framework 2023, pp.50–51](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- UFLI 8 steps (~30 min): 1 phonemic awareness; 2 visual drill; 3 auditory drill; 4 blending drill; 5 new concept; 6 word work; 7 irregular (heart) words; 8 connected text. — [third-party summary, Spelling Test Buddy](https://spellingtestbuddy.com/resources/ufli-scope-and-sequence/) (not verified against UFLI manual)
- Rosenshine's 10 principles: begin with short review; present new material in small steps with practice after each; ask many questions and check all responses; provide models; guide student practice; check for understanding; obtain a high success rate (optimal "about 80 percent"); provide scaffolds; require and monitor independent practice; engage in weekly and monthly review. — [Rosenshine 2012, American Educator](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf)
- RWI reading routine: each decodable book read 3 times (accuracy, fluency, storyteller voice). — [Oxford Owl](https://home.oxfordowl.co.uk/reading/reading-schemes-oxford-levels/read-write-inc-phonics-guide/)
- WWC on gradual release: reduce prompts over time (e.g., ask "What can you try?" rather than "sound it out"). — [WWC 2016, p.30](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf)

### Inferences
- A web app lesson template: (1) warm-up review of previously taught GPCs (speed-sound flash); (2) PA oral warm-up (blend/segment, 1–2 min); (3) new GPC introduction (sound, mouth cue, mnemonic, grapheme, upper/lower); (4) blending drill with new + old GPCs; (5) word building/chains (encode); (6) tricky word with irregular part highlighted; (7) decodable sentence/mini-book read (×2–3); (8) dictation of a sentence; (9) spaced review queue. Target ~80% success (Rosenshine).
- Durations: DfE ~10 min at start growing to ~1 hour/day by end of Reception (split); UFLI ~30 min. App sessions of 10–20 min with spaced review fit these.

### Gaps
- Archer & Hughes (2011) *Explicit Instruction* elements and Letters and Sounds 2007's "revisit/review, teach, practise, apply, assess" structure were not retrieved from primary sources this session. Official UFLI manual description of the 8 steps not verified.

## 8. Activity types: evidence for and against

### Takeaway
Strongly supported: phoneme segmentation with sound boxes linked to letters, word building/manipulation, left-to-right blending (sounding out or cumulative "chunking"), dictation, reading decodable connected text daily with repeated/partner/echo/choral reading. Discouraged: guessing from pictures, first letters or context; riddle-style hints; non-decodable levelled books; busy displays.

### Cited Findings
- Evidence levels (WWC 2016): Rec. 1 academic language/vocabulary — Minimal; Rec. 2 phonemic awareness linked to letters — Strong; Rec. 3 decoding, word parts, writing/recognising words — Strong; Rec. 4 daily connected text for accuracy/fluency/comprehension — Moderate. — [WWC 2016](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf)
- Elkonin boxes (WWC Example 2.3): boxes = phonemes; pull a disc or letter tile into each box while saying the word slowly; run finger under boxes and blend; start with continuous-sound words (e.g., fan); with letters use only taught GPCs; contrast minimal pairs (dog/dig). Example 3.4: building words with sound boxes. — [WWC 2016](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf)
- Blending (WWC Rec 3.1): left to right; start with familiar CVC; two routines — "chunking" (cumulative: add each successive sound to the previous chunk) or "sounding out" (each sound, then say all quickly); Examples 3.1–3.2 blend "hat" with pocket chart/letter tiles. — [WWC 2016, pp.23–24](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf)
- DfE decoding routine: see word → say each phoneme → blend (cat; sheep = /sh/ /ee/ /p/); encoding: hear → say → write graphemes (dog; goat). Pure sound pronunciation helps blending (Appendix 6). — [Reading Framework 2023, pp.45–46](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- Guessing discouraged: WWC "discourages teachers from allowing students to use guessing strategies... using beginning letters or pictures" and cautions against riddle hints ("What do you call the place where you live?" for h-o-m-e). — [WWC 2016, p.30](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf). DfE: decodable books mean children "are not forced to guess from pictures, the context, the first letters of a word or its shape"; levelled/colour-banded books expecting guessing from "the first letter, from pictures or context cues, or from syntax, should be avoided." — [Reading Framework 2023, pp.54, ~85](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- Decodable texts should "run alongside or a little behind the teaching of the GPCs"; children read a decodable text "most days". — [Reading Framework 2023, pp.54, 57](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- Fluency practices (WWC Rec 4): partner reading, choral reading, echo reading, repeated reading; texts at instructional level; preview hard words. — [WWC 2016, pp.33–35](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf)
- Environment: "highly decorated walls in primary schools undermine children's ability to concentrate"; phonics taught in a quiet space; display materials should match the programme. Audit item: "Teachers are aware of activities that might hinder children's progress." — [Reading Framework 2023, pp.53–54, 57](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf) (citing Fisher et al. 2014)
- DfE: no list-learning of HFWs (see §3). WWC, by contrast, suggests HFW word walls and flashcards. — [Reading Framework 2023](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf); [WWC 2016 Ex. 3.8–3.9](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf)
- Mnemonic picture for each letter (WWC Ex. 2.4, e.g., Pp with pig); Jolly Phonics uses actions; RWI uses picture mnemonics and Fred the frog. — [WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf); [Jolly map](https://henhamugley.essex.sch.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/Jolly-Phonics-progression-map-1.pdf); [Oxford Owl](https://home.oxfordowl.co.uk/reading/reading-schemes-oxford-levels/read-write-inc-phonics-guide/)
- DfE: avoid mixing programmes' terminology (e.g., "split digraph" vs "magic e") — "fidelity to the programme". — [Reading Framework 2023, p.47](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)

### Inferences
- App pitfalls: picture-word matching tasks where the picture gives the answer allow guessing without decoding; design so the picture is revealed after the child reads, or use distractors that share initial letters (e.g., cat/cap/cot) so the first letter alone fails.
- Word chains (change one letter: cat→cap→map→mop) operationalise WWC "word building/manipulation" and the dog/dig minimal-pair advice.
- "Maximise the number of words children read and spell" (DfE) argues against slow, low-yield game mechanics (long animations between items).

### Gaps
- No primary source found this session specifically evaluating "letter of the week" or craft activities; the claim they have limited value is not sourced here. Continuous vs successive blending comparative studies, and "final blending" terminology, not retrieved (WWC only describes both routines without ranking them). Word sorts not covered.

## 9. Fluency and comprehension strands alongside phonics

### Takeaway
Run oral language, read-aloud and vocabulary (Beck's Tier 2 "robust" instruction) from day one; fluency = accuracy + speed (prosody follows), developed through decodable re-reading and later repeated, echo, choral and partner reading.

### Cited Findings
- DfE fluency: measured as words read correctly per minute; national curriculum "accurately and speedily"; prosody unlikely without word-level accuracy/speed; "progressive" fluency at each stage; re-read decodable books to build fluency. — [Reading Framework 2023, Section 4, pp.60–64](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- DfE vocabulary: Beck's "second tier" words from picture books (e.g., bellowed, startled, barged, sneaked, grinned, dreadful, stomped, refused; explore, discovered, wondered, enormous...); "A robust approach to vocabulary involves directly explaining the meanings of words along with thought-provoking, playful, and interactive follow-up"; give examples in children's own lives ("Everyone was startled when the balloon popped"). Citation: Beck, McKeown & Kucan (2013) *Bringing Words to Life*. — [Reading Framework 2023, pp.35–36](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- DfE read-aloud: daily story time is a priority; repeated readings let children join in; exposure figures cited (never read to: 4,662 words vs daily: 296,660 words, per the cited 2019 study); core read-aloud stories plus teacher's choice. — [Reading Framework 2023, Section 2](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- DfE: decoding unfamiliar words gives teachers opportunities to explain meaning and build vocabulary. — [Reading Framework 2023, p.47](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/664f600c05e5fe28788fc437/The_reading_framework_.pdf)
- WWC Rec 1 (Minimal evidence): conversations supporting inferential language; explicit narrative language skills; academic vocabulary in context of reading. Rec 4: self-monitoring and self-correcting; reread sentence to check sense. — [WWC 2016](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf)
- RWI third read "storyteller voice" to show understanding. — [Oxford Owl](https://home.oxfordowl.co.uk/reading/reading-schemes-oxford-levels/read-write-inc-phonics-guide/)

### Inferences
- For ESL learners (e.g., Japanese children), the vocabulary/oral-language strand matters more than for native speakers: decodable words should be taught for meaning (picture after decoding), and a listening "story time" mode with Tier 2 words can run alongside.
- App fluency loop: each decodable mini-book read 3×; track words-correct-per-minute on short passages once CVC decoding is secure.

### Gaps
- Dialogic reading (Whitehurst, PEER/CROWD) and knowledge-building research not retrieved; AERO, Reading Rockets, Ehri phase theory, Moats, Kilpatrick (orthographic mapping) not fetched this session. No fluency norms (e.g., Hasbrouck & Tindal) retrieved.
