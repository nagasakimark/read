# Adapting a Systematic Phonics Curriculum for Japanese Elementary EFL Learners (ages 6-12)

Research done 2026-11-07. Sources were checked by fetching each page. Where only an abstract or a secondary summary could be read, the note says so. Many classic references (Swan & Smith *Learner English*, Vance 1987, Mann 1986 full text, Cheung & Slavin) could not be fetched directly. Claims that rest only on general knowledge appear under Inferences or Gaps, not as cited findings.

## 1. Japanese vs English phonology: what makes decoding and pronouncing English hard

### Takeaway
Japanese has five vowels, a mostly (C)V mora-based syllable structure, no /l/-/r/, /v/ or /θ ð/ contrasts, and sound changes before /i/ and /u/. Japanese listeners even *perceive* phantom vowels inside consonant clusters. So final consonants, consonant clusters and most English vowel contrasts are the main pronunciation and perception difficulties a phonics app has to plan around.

### Cited Findings
- Japanese has "only five basic vowel phonemes, and no diphthongs" (Okada 1991, cited). Learners merge pairs such as nought/note, cap/cup and lurk/lark, drop schwas, reduce diphthongs, and "may map their own L1 vowels over those of the L2" (Thompson 1987; Nishikiori 2007, cited) — [Lesley 2014, HPU TESOL Working Paper Series 12, pp.18-31](https://aws1.hpu.edu/research-publications/tesol-working-papers/2014/TESOL_WPS_2014_Lesley.pdf)
- /r/ and /l/ merge into a Japanese flap, "almost like a short d". /v/ tends to become [b]. /θ ð/ are replaced by /s z/ (or /ʃ dʒ/). /h/ before /u/ becomes a bilabial fricative ("foo" for *who*). /si ti tu di du zu/ become palatalised or affricated forms (shi, chi, tsu) (Thompson 1987; Shibatani 1987, cited) — [Lesley 2014](https://aws1.hpu.edu/research-publications/tesol-working-papers/2014/TESOL_WPS_2014_Lesley.pdf)
- Final consonants get an extra vowel, "oftentimes unintentionally": map → /mapɯ/, end → /endo/ (Carruthers 2006, cited). Consonant clusters are broken up with inserted vowels that follow katakana CV patterns (Kaneko 2006, cited) — [Lesley 2014](https://aws1.hpu.edu/research-publications/tesol-working-papers/2014/TESOL_WPS_2014_Lesley.pdf)
- Japanese is mora-timed and English is stress-timed. Splitting loanwords into morae makes learner English "appear over-enunciated" (Cross 2002, cited). Japanese lacks word-level stress and reduced forms, so English suprasegmentals must be "consciously learnt and practiced" (Thompson 1987, cited). Japanese /i/ and /u/ often devoice, and its /u/ is unrounded (Vance 1987, cited) — [Lesley 2014](https://aws1.hpu.edu/research-publications/tesol-working-papers/2014/TESOL_WPS_2014_Lesley.pdf)
- **Perception, not just production:** Japanese listeners reported hearing a vowel (usually [u]) inside VCCV clusters where none was present, and found VCCV vs VCuCV (*ebzo* vs *ebuzo*) hard to tell apart. French listeners had no such problem, but struggled with vowel-length contrasts that Japanese uses — [Dupoux, Kakehi, Hirose, Pallier & Mehler, "Epenthetic vowels in Japanese: A perceptual illusion?" (preprint 1998; published J. Exp. Psych.: HPP 1999)](https://web-archive.southampton.ac.uk/cogprints.org/747/index.html)
- In katakana phonology, *cold, called, code* and *cord* "are all pronounced kōdō". In a small listening test, Japanese university students averaged 9.44/20 identifying words read by a native speaker (about 5.92 after correcting for guessing), and often heard English sounds as Japanese categories (e.g., /v/ as /b/) — [Beasom 2011, Accents Asia 4(1), 36-59](https://accentsasia.org/issues/4-1/beasom.pdf) (a small, mostly diagnostic study; some evidence is anecdotal)

### Inferences
- The perceptual epenthesis finding (Dupoux) means children may *hear* "cat" as "catto" and "stop" as "sutoppu". Blending and segmenting activities therefore need clean, unreleased or lightly released final consonants in the audio, and should explicitly contrast "cat" vs "catto". Vowel length is something Japanese children perceive well, so length-based teaching cues (e.g., long ee vs short i) work with a native strength. Vowel *quality* contrasts (/æ/-/ʌ/-/ɑ/) do not, and need extra listening practice.
- Expect particular trouble with the early phonics set: short a/u/o (cat/cut/cot), short i vs ee, oo/ʊ vs uː, r/l, v/b, f/h before u (fun/hun), th, si/shi (sit vs shit is a genuine embarrassment risk), and every CVC final consonant.
- Japanese uses vowel length contrastively (French listeners struggled with *ebuuzo*), so Japanese children should handle "long vowel" labels readily. But English "long vowels" are often diphthongs (/eɪ aɪ oʊ/), which Japanese lacks, so they may come out as monophthongs (e.g., "cake" → keeki).

### Gaps
- Could not fetch Swan & Smith (*Learner English*, Thompson chapter) or Vance (*The Sounds of Japanese*) directly; they are cited here only through Lesley 2014.
- /f/ vs Japanese [ɸ] (fu) and /si/ → [ɕi] are described in Lesley only indirectly. A primary phonetic reference was not fetched.
- Almost all of the perception and production evidence comes from adults or university students. I found no study of perceptual epenthesis or vowel discrimination in Japanese *children* aged 6-12.

## 2. Katakana loanwords and katakana pronunciation guides

### Takeaway
Katakana can represent only CV morae and five vowels, so it collapses English contrasts and builds in epenthesis. Beasom argues it contributes to communication failure, although it is widely used in Japanese EFL materials. The evidence against katakana guides is largely argument plus small diagnostic studies, not controlled trials.

### Cited Findings
- Katakana is "often ill-suited or inadequate to the task of representing English phones". It is still used in Ministry-approved textbooks and major publishers' materials to explain English pronunciation, and is valued as "a welcome and more accessible alternative to the International Phonetic Alphabet" (Mizui 2001, cited). Its use is "largely legitimized by its cultural and educational role" — [Beasom 2011](https://accentsasia.org/issues/4-1/beasom.pdf)
- Beasom concludes that learners need explicit phonological instruction and awareness of the phonological pitfalls that katakana hides — [Beasom 2011](https://accentsasia.org/issues/4-1/beasom.pdf)
- Loanword adaptation into katakana is "based on phonetic approximation rather than a preservation of the phonological categories of the source language" (Kaneko 2006, cited) — [Lesley 2014](https://aws1.hpu.edu/research-publications/tesol-working-papers/2014/TESOL_WPS_2014_Lesley.pdf)

### Inferences
- An app should never show katakana readings of English words (e.g., キャット under *cat*). Audio is the pronunciation model, and the spelling is the visual representation. Japanese can still be used for instructions and meanings.
- Katakana loanwords have two sides. Many picturable CVC words are already known as loanwords (バス bus, カップ cup, ペン pen, ベッド bed, トップ top), which helps meaning: the child knows what "bus" means, so decoding does not turn into "barking at print". But the child's stored katakana form (basu) competes with the English form. Loanword targets should be used, with deliberate "English sound vs katakana sound" contrast moments, rather than avoided.
- Watch for false friends and shifted meanings (e.g., マンション = apartment, not mansion). This matters more for later vocabulary than for CVC words.

### Gaps
- Did not fetch research on the size of the loanword vocabulary or loanword cognates as an L2 vocabulary aid (e.g., Daulton's *Japan's Built-in Lexicon of English-based Loanwords*, 2008). Name it only as a lead.
- Found no controlled study comparing children taught with vs without katakana pronunciation aids.

## 3. Phonological awareness: mora-based segmentation, teachability, kana/romaji transfer

### Takeaway
Kana literacy pushes Japanese children toward *mora* segmentation, and phoneme awareness is weak without alphabetic literacy. However, phoneme awareness can be trained, including in Japanese children, and training that makes use of the kana grid's consonant-vowel structure worked best in one study. Romaji is a known source of letter-sound interference when reading unfamiliar English words.

### Cited Findings
- Mann (1986), "Phonological awareness: the role of reading experience" (Cognition 24), is the foundational study. Its abstract could not be fetched. Spagnoletti et al. (1989), "inspired by Mann (1986)", found Japanese first-graders learning a syllabic script performed relatively weakly at syllable- and phoneme-level analysis. This supports the view that segmental awareness is closely tied to alphabetic literacy. The children also seemed to rely on spelling-based (kana) strategies — [Spagnoletti, Morais, Alegria & Dominicy 1989, Reading and Writing 1(3), 221-244](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00377644); [Mann 1986 PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3791922/) (record only, abstract not retrieved)
- As 4-6-year-olds learned kana, their conscious segmentation of words containing special morae (CVN, e.g., "pan" = pa-n) shifted from a mix of syllable and mora units to mostly mora-based units. A less conscious target-monitoring task showed the same shift — [Inagaki, Hatano & Otake 2000, J. Exp. Child Psychology 75(1), 70-91](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/68429415) (abstract only)
- Some kana-only Japanese 4th graders could manipulate phonemes to a meaningful degree. In two training studies, phoneme-awareness training worked, and worked *best* when it explicitly referred to how the kana matrix is organised (the consonant rows and vowel columns of the 50-sound table) — [Goetry, Urbain, Morais & Kolinsky 2005, Applied Psycholinguistics 26(2), 285-309](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/abs/paths-to-phonemic-awareness-in-japanese-evidence-from-a-training-study/CCAB2224CBCB0984EDA178D78B8BEA50) (abstract only)
- Twelve sessions of synthetic phonics with 17 Japanese **2nd graders** improved phonemic awareness on Phoneme Oddity tasks (initial and final sound). The effect did not differ by task type. There was no control group, the posttest came 3 days after the sessions ended, and n = 17 — [Sasaki 2024, Journal of Elementary Education and Curriculum 12, 41-49 (Hiroshima Univ.)](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/seec/12/0/12_41/_article/-char/en)
- Tamai Allen pilot study: 59 Tokyo children aged 7-11 with one 60-minute English lesson a week. Reading correlated with age (r = .63), spelling (r = .62) and onset/rime awareness (r = .53). In the path analysis, sound awareness had a small direct effect (.22), spelling .43, and age was "the strongest factor". Alliteration was easier than rhyme detection. The author found pseudo-word spelling tests "counterproductive for young foreign language learners" — [Tamai Allen, TUJ Working Papers in Applied Linguistics vol. 14 (year not stated on page)](https://www.tuj.ac.jp/tesol/publications/working-papers/vol-14/tamaiallen)
- **Romaji:** Japanese students generally apply a Romaji strategy when reading unfamiliar English words. Intermediate learners depend on it entirely, advanced learners overcorrect, and the early groups generally do not know English spelling-to-sound rules. The author recommends teaching English GPCs early — [Doki 1996, M.A. thesis, Univ. of Illinois](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/132958)
- Phonics with careful attention to the differences between Japanese and English was effective for two adolescents with specific English-learning difficulties (pre/post test on 70 unlearned words, no control group) — [Kamioka & Suzuki 2019, ARELE 30, 257-269](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/arele/30/0/30_257/_article/-char/en)

### Inferences
- Activities that target onsets work better than rhyme-based ones for Japanese children. Alliteration was easier than rhyme, and English rhyme games assume rich oral English exposure. Start with initial-phoneme isolation, then final phoneme, then medial vowel. Treat rhyme/rime awareness as a later, supported skill.
- Use the kana grid as a bridge. か = /k/ + /a/, and the か row shares /k/. That is a culturally familiar way to show that a "letter sound" is a consonant without a vowel (Goetry et al. 2005). This also directly attacks the epenthesis habit: /k/ is not "ku".
- Romaji helps with letter shapes, left-to-right reading and some consonant values (b, d, k, m, n, p, s, t mostly match). It interferes with vowels (romaji a = /a/, but English short a = /æ/; romaji u = /ɯ/, but English short u = /ʌ/), with CV chunking (reading "cat" as "ka-to"), and with letters like r, f, h, j, c, x, q. The app should state these conflicts explicitly (perhaps "ローマ字の a とちがうよ!") rather than ignore them.
- Avoid nonsense-word reading and spelling tests in early stages, or use them sparingly (Tamai Allen). This departs from UK-style phonics screening practice.

### Gaps
- Could not verify the claim that romaji is taught in grade 3 kokugo from a fetched MEXT source in this session, although it is widely reported. Treat it as unverified here.
- Found no controlled study isolating whether *grade-3 romaji instruction* helps or hinders later English phonics in children.
- Allen-Tamai's larger KAKEN-funded projects on literacy in public elementary schools exist (KAKEN 16520348, 20520546), but their detailed results were not fetched.

## 4. MEXT Course of Study (2017 revision, in force from 2020) on letters, sounds and reading

### Takeaway
MEXT requires grade 5-6 pupils to recognise printed letters and "pronounce their readings", and to understand the meaning of simple words and expressions *already well familiarised through sound*. Oral-first is official policy. The commentary (解説) states that English letters have both a name and a sound. Systematic decoding is not mandated at elementary level.

### Cited Findings
- Grades 5-6 English (subject), Reading goals: "ア 活字体で書かれた文字を識別し，その読み方を発音することができるようにする。" (identify letters written in print and be able to pronounce how they are read) and "イ 音声で十分に慣れ親しんだ簡単な語句や基本的な表現の意味が分かるようにする。" (understand the meaning of simple words and basic expressions *sufficiently familiarised through sound*) — [Course of Study, jp-cos machine-readable edition](https://jp-cos.github.io/en/82H/12D2200000000)
- The MEXT commentary (外国語活動・外国語編 p.78), as quoted by Allen-Tamai: "英語の文字には、名称と音がある。" (English letters have a name and a sound). The sound reading of a letter is a "手掛かり" (clue) for pronouncing words. Allen-Tamai argues the most important thing in elementary literacy is teaching "この文字と音の関係（alphabetic principle）". She contrasts whole-word methods with decoding and favours decoding. She considers copying letters without sound "あまり意味がない" — [アレン玉井光江, ARCLE/Benesse report (c. 2018, transition year 1)](https://www.benesse.jp/berd/special/arcle/report/pdf/2019_01.pdf)

### Inferences
- An app aligned with MEXT should (a) teach letter names *and* letter sounds, (b) use words children have already met orally in Let's Try / New Horizon Elementary for decoding practice, and (c) always pair print with audio ("音声で十分に慣れ親しんだ" is the gatekeeper). This fits well with a phonics app that only asks children to decode words they have heard and seen pictured.
- Grades 3-4 (Let's Try, Foreign Language *Activities*) focus on letter recognition and names. Letter sounds and simple decoding fit grades 5-6 official goals. A self-study app could reasonably start sounds earlier, as Sasaki 2024 did with 2nd graders.

### Gaps
- The exact grade 3-4 Foreign Language Activities text on letters (recognising upper- and lowercase letters and pronouncing their names) was not fetched here.
- The statement that formal spelling-sound rules (発音と綴りの関係) are treated in junior high was not verified from the fetched MEXT text.
- MEXT's English translation of the Course of Study was not retrieved.

## 5. EFL/ESL phonics evidence generally; oral language and vocabulary as the bottleneck

### Takeaway
For second-language learners, decoding and spelling can reach native-like levels with instruction, but comprehension lags and is tied to oral language and vocabulary. Methods that work for native speakers work for L2 learners with smaller effects and "adjustments are needed".

### Cited Findings
- National Literacy Panel on Language-Minority Children and Youth (August & Shanahan, eds., 2006), as summarised by Escamilla: language-minority students often match monolingual peers on word-level skills (decoding, spelling), but text-level skills "rarely reach levels equal to monolingual English speakers". L2 oral proficiency is closely related to L2 literacy, especially comprehension (correlational). Instructional strategies that work for native speakers help English learners, but "the effect sizes are lower and adjustments are needed" — [Escamilla 2009, Journal of Literacy Research 41, 432-452](https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/upload/publications/45/pdf/10862960903340165.pdf)
- Note on context: the NLP covers language-minority children *immersed* in English (ESL in the US), not EFL children with about 1-2 hours of weekly exposure. Japanese elementary pupils have far less oral input, so the oral-language bottleneck is probably more severe.

### Inferences
- "Barking at print" is a major risk. A Japanese child can decode *rug, jog, fin* perfectly well and not know what they mean. Decodable word lists should be filtered to words that are (a) picturable, (b) in Let's Try / New Horizon Elementary vocabulary or common loanwords, and (c) introduced orally with picture and audio *before* the child decodes them.
- Every decoding item should end in a meaning check (tap the picture) so that decoding is tied to comprehension.

### Gaps
- Could not fetch Lesaux & Siegel (2003) or Cheung & Slavin syntheses directly. Lesaux & Siegel's finding that ESL children in Canada with strong phonological instruction reached parity on word reading is known but unverified in this session.
- Found no EFL-specific meta-analysis of phonics for East Asian elementary learners in this session.

## 6. Recommended adaptations vs what to keep; decoding vs accent (intelligibility)

### Takeaway
Keep the systematic GPC sequence, the decodable-text principle and blending/segmenting. Change the input: more listening first, audio-led minimal pairs, clipped consonant sounds and clean final consonants, onset before rime, Japanese-language scaffolding, vocabulary restricted to known or picturable words, no katakana readings. Aim for intelligibility rather than native accent. In Jenkins' Lingua Franca Core, final-vowel epenthesis is tolerated, but consonant deletion is not.

### Cited Findings
- Munro & Derwing (1995) separated accentedness, comprehensibility and intelligibility as partly independent dimensions. A 2021 conceptual replication (Huensch & Nagle, Language Learning) confirmed "the robustness of partial independence among speech dimensions" — [FORRT replication record for Munro & Derwing 1995, Language Learning 45(1), 73-97](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1111/j.1467-1770.1995.tb00963.x/)
- Jenkins' Lingua Franca Core (2006 summary) *includes* consonant sounds (with th-substitution and dark-l tolerated), aspiration of initial /p t k/, avoiding consonant deletion in clusters, vowel *length* distinctions, and nuclear stress. It *excludes* vowel quality, weak forms, connected speech (elision, assimilation), word stress, pitch movement and stress-timed rhythm. Example: the /r/-/l/ distinction (read/lead) is core, but adding a final vowel (/ri:do/) is tolerated — [hellog summary of Jenkins 2006, in Rubdy & Saraceni (eds.) *English in the World*](https://helhoard.com/2022-06-17.html)
- The Japanese elementary phonics evidence is small but positive: Sasaki 2024 (n = 17, grade 2, phonemic awareness gains); Tamai Allen pilot (correlational); Goetry et al. 2005 (training works, especially with kana-grid scaffolding) — sources as in section 3
- Doki 1996 recommends teaching English GPCs early to counter the Romaji strategy — [Doki 1996](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/132958)

### Inferences (recommendations for the app; these are interpretation, not tested findings)
**Keep (from native-speaker systematic synthetic phonics):**
- An explicit, cumulative GPC sequence (e.g., s a t p i n → ...), blending and segmenting, decodable words and sentences only from taught GPCs, a small set of tricky/high-frequency words taught explicitly, and frequent review. This is supported by general phonics evidence and by MEXT's "names and sounds" position. There is no evidence that Japanese children need a different *order* of GPCs, though the order could be tuned (see below).

**Change or add:**
1. **Oral/listening phase before each word set:** picture + audio + meaning (Japanese gloss if needed) before decoding (MEXT 音声で十分に慣れ親しんだ; NLP oral-language link).
2. **Audio-first minimal-pair discrimination games** for high-risk contrasts in the order they appear: short a/u/o (cat/cut/cot), i/ee (ship/sheep), r/l, b/v, s/sh before i, f/h (fun/hun), th. Use native-speaker audio from several speakers for perceptual training (inference; the high-variability training literature was not fetched).
3. **Clipped ("pure") consonant sounds** (/m/ not "mu", /t/ not "to") with no added schwa or Japanese vowel. Show the kana-grid analogy (か = k + a) to explain consonants without vowels (Goetry et al. 2005).
4. **Final-consonant focus:** explicit "cat vs catto" contrasts and segmenting tasks that ask "what's the last sound?" (Dupoux perceptual epenthesis; Sasaki's End Oddity task was trainable).
5. **Onset before rime:** alliteration and initial-sound games before rhyme. Rhyme-dependent activities (rhyme families, nursery-rhyme knowledge) come later and are supported (Tamai Allen: alliteration easier than rhyme).
6. **Explicit romaji-conflict callouts** for vowels and r/f/h/c/x/q (Doki 1996).
7. **No katakana pronunciation guides.** Japanese for instructions, hints and meanings only (Beasom 2011).
8. **Vocabulary selection:** known oral vocabulary first (Let's Try / New Horizon Elementary words), picturable items, and useful loanwords used *deliberately*, with an English-vs-katakana sound contrast moment. Avoid obscure CVC words that only make sense to native speakers (e.g., *nab, vat, cog*) except as clearly marked "silly words".
9. **Limited nonsense-word testing**, or nonsense words framed as alien/monster names (as in Teach Your Monster to Read), with the meaning check skipped for those (Tamai Allen caution).
10. **Pacing:** slower introduction and more review cycles than UK native programmes. Exposure is about 1-2 lessons a week in school, versus daily phonics plus an English-speaking environment for native children (inference; I found no study setting a pace).
11. **Pronunciation goal = intelligibility:** prioritise LFC-core items (consonant contrasts including r/l, final consonants present, vowel *length*). Do not penalise imperfect th, schwa, weak forms or connected speech in early decoding. If the app uses speech recognition, set tolerant thresholds and do not mark accented but intelligible output as wrong (Munro & Derwing; Jenkins).
12. **Diphthong awareness** when "long vowels" are introduced (a_e = /eɪ/, not "ē"), because Japanese lacks diphthongs (Lesley 2014).

### Gaps
- No controlled trial was found that compares a *Japanese-adapted* phonics programme with an unadapted native one. Every adaptation above is inferred from contrastive phonology, small Japanese studies and L2 literacy research in general.
- High-variability phonetic training for /r/-/l/ (Logan, Lively & Pisoni; Bradlow et al., mostly with adults) was not fetched, and its applicability to children is unverified here.
- Speech-recognition accuracy for Japanese children's English was not researched.
