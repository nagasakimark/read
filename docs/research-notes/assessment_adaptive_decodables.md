# Assessment, Mastery, Adaptive Sequencing/Spaced Review, and Decodable-Text Design for an Early-Reading Web App

Scope note: research done 2026-11-07, about 25 tool calls. Several sources (PMC, some PDFs) were blocked or only partly readable. Anything not checked in this session is listed under Gaps, or flagged "unverified". Empirical findings and design inferences are kept apart. **Conventions (not evidence)** are marked as such.

---

## 1. Assessment and mastery: how can an app reliably know a child has learned each reading skill?

### Takeaway
The standard early-reading measures (DIBELS 8, the UK Phonics Screening Check) test decoding with **pseudo-words read aloud**, and they use timed fluency, not accuracy alone. Mastery learning has a solid meta-analytic base (ES about 0.52; higher mastery standards work better). But the exact criterion numbers (80/90/100%, "3 in a row", "<2 s") are mostly practitioner conventions with thin direct evidence. Browser speech recognition is not reliable enough to grade children's L2 oral reading on its own.

### Cited Findings

**Established measures**
- DIBELS 8th Edition (University of Oregon, document dated 13 Aug 2018) has these subtests: Letter Naming Fluency (K–1), Phonemic Segmentation Fluency (K–1), Nonsense Word Fluency (K–3, measures "decoding nonsense words (phonics)"), Word Reading Fluency (K–3, new; real words out of context), Oral Reading Fluency (rate and accuracy in connected text) and Maze (comprehension; grades 2–8 from 2019). — [DIBELS 8 "What's New" (2018)](https://dibels.amplify.com/docs/dibels/Whats-New-DIBELS-Eighth-Edition.pdf)
- DIBELS 8 NWF spelling patterns are chosen by frequency and are phonetically regular. Patterns go beyond CVC as grades rise (VC and others in Grade 1, more complex patterns in Grades 2–3). Scoring changed from "whole words read" to **"words recoded correctly" (WRC)**, so a child who sounds out and then blends still gets credit. PSF words come from the 2,500 most frequent words and start with two-phoneme items. LNF samples letters by print frequency. — [DIBELS 8 "What's New" (2018)](https://dibels.amplify.com/docs/dibels/Whats-New-DIBELS-Eighth-Edition.pdf)
- The DIBELS 8 document says past maze measures tapped mainly **low-level comprehension**. — [DIBELS 8 "What's New" (2018)](https://dibels.amplify.com/docs/dibels/Whats-New-DIBELS-Eighth-Edition.pdf)
- UK Phonics Screening Check (England, end of Year 1): pupils read **40 items aloud** to a teacher, "a mix of real words and pseudo-words". Each item scores 1 mark, and the total is compared with a threshold mark that DfE publishes on GOV.UK after the check window closes. The check is described as an assessment of "phonics decoding ability". — [STA Phonics technical specification (2024, v1.0)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/662b8448fddcc9e7ab225285/Phonics_2024_technical_specification_v1.0.pdf); materials index: [GOV.UK PSC 2024 materials](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/phonics-screening-check-2024-materials)
- **Unverified in this session:** the brief's details (20 pseudo-words, threshold 32, alien pictures marking pseudo-words, a Section 1 / Section 2 split) are widely reported, e.g. [Tes, "Phonics screening check 2024: all you need to know"](https://www.tes.com/magazine/need-know-phonics-check). The technical spec I fetched does not state them.

**Mastery learning evidence**
- Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990), *Review of Educational Research* 60(2): 108 controlled evaluations. The mean effect on end-of-instruction exams was **0.52 SD** (about 50th to 70th percentile): Keller PSI 0.48, Bloom's Learning for Mastery 0.59. Programs with a **high mastery standard were more effective** than those with lower standards. In 11 studies with follow-up tests about 8 weeks later, the effect was 0.71, against 0.60 at the end of instruction. The median time cost was only about 4% more. Less able students gained 0.61 and more able students 0.40; this difference was not statistically significant. — [Kulik et al. 1990 (PDF)](https://scispace.com/pdf/effectiveness-of-mastery-learning-programs-a-meta-analysis-45bgwh1byb.pdf)
- Mastery criteria and maintenance: a 2020 descriptive review in *Behavior Analysis in Practice* found that only **two studies** had systematically tested how a mastery-criterion dimension affects maintenance (Fuller & Fienup 2018; Richling, Williams & Carr). It also found that practitioners often use criterion levels lower than those found to reliably produce maintenance. Researchers tended to use higher accuracy over fewer observations; practitioners used lower accuracy over more observations. — [McDougale et al. 2020, Behav Anal Pract 13:402–410](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40617-019-00365-2)

**Fluency and automaticity**
- Incremental rehearsal (IR), from school-psychology practice, defines a "known" item as one answered correctly **within 2 seconds**. — [Tucker & Burns 2016, NASP Communiqué 44(6)](https://nj.dyslexiaida.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/18/2025/10/Tucker-and-Burns-CQ_44_6.pdf)

**Guessing and response format**
- Pelánek (2016) notes that for multiple-choice items with *k* options, Elo/IRT models need a **shifted logistic with a guessing floor of 1/k**. In other words, multiple-choice success is contaminated by chance, and the model must discount it. — [Pelánek 2016, Computers & Education (author PDF)](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf)

**Speech recognition for oral reading**
- Scientific Learning's Reading Assistant (2008 briefing, citing Adams 2006) was compared with teacher judgments on 349 passages read by 153 students in grades 2–6. The software agreed with teachers on about **95% of words** (teachers agreed with each other on about 98%). False positive and false negative rates were each about 1–2.6% by grade, with similar results for a 6th-grade ELL subgroup. Note: this is a vendor document, the passages were constrained (known text), and the engine was tuned for the task. — [Scientific Learning, Reading Assistant sensitivity briefing (Mar 2008)](https://www.scilearn.com/wp-content/uploads/30389RASensitivity.pdf)
- Young L2 learners (Swiss grades 4–6, ages 9–14, 85 h of spontaneous English): off-the-shelf ASR had **WER 0.25–0.38** (Whisper Large 0.25). These systems also **"tend to correct the speakers' errors, which renders giving corrective feedback impossible."** Whisper-Large silently corrected about 26% of frequent learner errors. A fine-tuned model preserved more errors (83%). CTC models without a language model preserve errors better than Whisper. — [Michot et al. 2024, arXiv:2406.03235](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2406.03235)
- Web Speech API (MDN): recognition is **server-based by default**; audio is sent to a web service and does not work offline. Many users are uncomfortable with that. An on-device option exists (`processLocally = true`), but it needs a per-language pack download and is not available on all hardware. Accuracy fails on uncommon words and words that sound alike. Phrase boosting (`SpeechRecognitionPhrase`, boost 0–10) can help. The `nomatch` event is unreliable because the engine usually returns *something*. — [MDN, Using the Web Speech API (accessed 2026-11)](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Web_Speech_API/Using_the_Web_Speech_API)

### Inferences (design recommendations, not evidence)
- **Use a ladder of evidence per grapheme–phoneme correspondence (GPC) or skill:** (1) recognition (hear sound, pick grapheme from a set), (2) recall/production (see grapheme, child produces or selects the sound; or hear sound, child builds or types the grapheme), (3) application (blend or build whole words with that GPC), (4) **transfer** (decode unseen pseudo-words containing the GPC, following the PSC and NWF logic), (5) automaticity (accurate *and* fast, e.g. under about 2 s, borrowing IR's "known" definition), (6) retention (still correct after a delay of at least 1 day). Mark a skill "mastered" only after stages 4–6, not after recognition.
- **Pseudo-words are the best available in-app check of decoding transfer**, because they cannot be memorized as sight words. Present them as "alien/monster names", as the PSC and Teach Your Monster to Read do. This framing also suits Japanese EFL children, for whom many real English words are unfamiliar anyway; for them a real word works almost like a pseudo-word.
- **Guessing:** with 3 options, chance is 33% per trial. Getting 5 of 5 right by chance is (1/3)^5, about 0.4%; with 4 options it is (1/4)^5, about 0.1%. 4 of 5 correct with 3 options is still about 4.5% by chance. So a rule like "5 consecutive correct, at least 3–4 options, plausible lures" makes chance mastery unlikely. **Prefer constructed responses** (drag letters to build a word, spelling) for the stages that count as mastery evidence; the chance rate there is near 0. Design lures from known confusions (b/d, short vowels e/i, and for Japanese learners l/r, th/s, vowel epenthesis), so that wrong answers are informative.
- **Criteria for an app (CONVENTIONS, tune with data):** at least 90% accuracy over the last 10 or more attempts, *and* at least 1 correct in a later session at least 24 h after (a "multiple-day" criterion), *and* a median response under about 2 s on recall items. Kulik supports "high standard"; nothing found supports a specific number.
- **Speech recognition:** do not use the browser's Web Speech API as a pass/fail grader for decoding. It is server-based, has privacy issues for school children, silently corrects errors, and handles Japanese-accented child English poorly. If used at all, make it a low-stakes "talk to the monster" practice feature with generous acceptance, phrase-boosted to the target word list, and never the sole mastery gate. Prefer tap-to-build and choose-from-audio tasks for scoring. Optionally record audio locally for a teacher to listen to.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a primary source on Acadience, CTOPP-2, or the published validity/reliability of NWF or the PSC. I also did not retrieve criticisms of pseudo-word tests (e.g. that the PSC penalizes good readers who "real-word-ise" pseudo-words, or teaching to the test). These need follow-up.
- Fuller & Fienup (2018) and Richling et al. were not read directly (PMC blocked). Their reported finding is that higher criterion levels and multiple-session criteria improve maintenance, but it is not verified here.
- Precision-teaching fluency aims (e.g., letter-sound "see-say" frequency aims of 60–100 per minute) and Hudson/Lane fluency work were not retrieved. No evidence-based latency cutoff for letter-sound automaticity was found; the 2 s figure is an IR convention.
- Accuracy data for Amira, Google Read Along, Microsoft Reading Coach, or Project LISTEN (Mostow) were not retrieved in this session.
- Bloom (1984, the 2-sigma problem) and Guskey were not retrieved.

## 2. Adaptive sequencing and spaced review rules

### Takeaway
Simple, data-light models work well enough for a children's phonics app: Leitner-style boxes or an Elo/half-life model with item selection aimed at about 75% success, and interspersal of a new item among many known ones (incremental rehearsal, about 1 unknown to 8–9 knowns). The paper behind Duolingo's half-life regression shows big prediction gains over Leitner, but it needs large datasets.

### Cited Findings
- **Half-life regression (HLR)**, Settles & Meeder, ACL 2016: recall probability p = 2^(−Δ/h), where Δ is the lag since last practice and h is the half-life, estimated as h = 2^(Θ·x) from features (e.g., counts of right and wrong answers). The model was trained on 12.9 million student-word traces. Mean absolute error was 0.128, against 0.235 for Leitner and 0.445 for Pimsleur, though AUC was about equal (0.538 vs 0.542). In live A/B tests, HLR versus Leitner had small effects on overall daily retention (+0.3%) and lowered practice-session retention (−7.3%). — [Settles & Meeder 2016 (PDF)](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/P16-1174.pdf); [code/data](https://www.github.com/duolingo/halflife-regression)
- **Elo for adaptive education** (Pelánek 2016, *Computers & Education*, doi:10.1016/j.compedu.2016.03.017): P(correct) is a logistic function of (skill − difficulty), and both are updated online after each answer. The paper recommends an uncertainty function U(n) = a/(1+bn) with a = 1 and b = 0.05 instead of a fixed K. About **10 answers per student** gave reasonable skill estimates; about **100 students** were needed for good item difficulty estimates. Simple percent-correct gives biased difficulty estimates under adaptive selection. An Elo-based variant (PFAE, with asymmetric updates) outperformed PFA and **Bayesian Knowledge Tracing** in prediction (Papoušek et al. 2014). Elo is suited to low-stakes practice in simple domains (vocabulary, basic skills). Item selection can **target a success rate (~75% in the Outline Maps system)**. — [Pelánek 2016 author PDF](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf)
- **Incremental rehearsal** (Tucker & Burns 2016): each unknown item is rehearsed inside a series of **8–9 known items**, in an expanding sequence. When a new unknown is introduced, the oldest known item is retired. Children recalled about 90% of items after 1 day (Nist & Joseph 2008) and about two-thirds after 1 month (MacQuarrie et al. 2002). Other flashcard methods ranged from 23% to 76%. IR takes 5–15 minutes versus 3–5 for drill. **Stop rule:** if the child suddenly misses previously known items, stop and teach no new items (fatigue). **Error rule:** if the child errs on the unknown item, model the answer and restart the sequence with it. — [Tucker & Burns 2016](https://nj.dyslexiaida.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/18/2025/10/Tucker-and-Burns-CQ_44_6.pdf)

### Inferences (design recommendations)
- **Recommended architecture for a static site** (no server, little data): store per-item state in localStorage, using either (a) a Leitner box with expanding intervals (e.g., same session, next session, 1 d, 3 d, 7 d, 14 d — CONVENTION), or (b) a per-item half-life with HLR's p = 2^(−Δ/h), doubling h on correct and shrinking it on error. Schedule an item when predicted recall drops below about 0.8–0.9. The HLR paper shows a trained model beats Leitner, but a hand-tuned half-life is effectively Leitner with continuous intervals; there is no data to train on at launch.
- **Session composition (CONVENTION grounded in IR and Elo):** aim for about 75–85% expected success overall. Use a **ratio of roughly 1 new to 4–9 known/review items**: IR's 1:8–9 at the start for struggling or young learners, loosening to about 1:3–4 for confident ones. Introduce no more than about 2–3 new GPCs per session, and stop introducing new items when errors on known items rise (IR fatigue rule).
- **Elo for difficulty calibration:** use it once usage exists (about 100 or more children). Before that, seed difficulty from the phonics sequence position.
- **Errors and remediation:** (1) an immediate corrective model (play the sound, show the mouth/grapheme, retry); (2) re-queue the missed item within the same session after 2–4 intervening items; (3) if an item fails twice within a session, or the child fails a review after a delay, drop it back to the "teach" stage (re-teach animation) and reset its interval; (4) track confusion pairs (e.g., b/d), add targeted contrast drills, and use the confused grapheme as the lure.
- **Children:** keep the model invisible. Show progress as collectibles and worlds, not percentages. Keep sessions short (5–10 min). The 2 s latency rule should only kick in after accuracy is established, so slow but accurate children are not penalized early.

### Gaps
- FSRS and SM-2 specifics, and any studies of spaced-repetition algorithms with young children (5–8), were not retrieved. I found no evidence on which algorithm is best for children specifically.
- BKT parameter guidance for literacy (e.g., the 0.95 mastery probability convention) was not retrieved from a primary source.
- I found no direct evidence on how intelligent tutoring or literacy software (Lexia, Amira, Teach Your Monster to Read, Duolingo ABC) triggers re-teaching; their internal rules are proprietary or were not found.
- "Interspersal" ratio research beyond IR (e.g., Burns' 2005+ studies on 50% vs 90% known) was not retrieved.

## 3. Framework for decodable stories aligned to a phonics sequence

### Takeaway
Decodable texts reliably improve **immediate decoding accuracy and use of decoding strategies**. Evidence for long-term gains is mixed, and reviewers warn against judging texts on decodability alone. A common *convention* is that texts should be close to fully decodable from taught GPCs plus a small set of explicitly taught "tricky" words, read repeatedly, and phased out once the core code is secure.

### Cited Findings
- Cheatham & Allor (2012), *Reading and Writing* 25:2223–2246, reviewed the evidence on decodability. Decodable text gives **immediate benefits, especially in accuracy**, and makes students more likely to use a decoding strategy. The authors argue early text should meet **multiple criteria**, with decodability as just one, and caution against designing text on decodability alone. — [Reading Rockets summary of Cheatham & Allor 2012](https://www.readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/influence-decodability-early-reading-text-reading-achievement-review); [full text PDF](https://virtualeduc.com/v7/resources/data/RF3.3/CheathamandAllor.pdf)
- England's Reading Framework (DfE, July 2023) supports decodable books for practising taught sounds, building accuracy, fluency and confidence, and for continuing systematic phonics for older pupils who cannot yet decode. — [Collins summary of the Reading Framework 2023 (June 2024)](https://collins.co.uk/blogs/the-book-squad-blog/the-reading-framework-2023-a-summary). (The summary does not state the "fully decodable / matched to programme" wording; the primary DfE document was not fetched.)
- MetaMetrics publishes "Lexile Decodable" tools for measuring decodability of early texts, so a commercial decodability metric exists. — [MetaMetrics Lexile Decodable Tools (PDF)](https://www2.metametricsinc.com/hubfs/Lexile-Decodable-Tools.pdf?hsLang=en) (not read in detail)
- UNC's TRI (Allor and colleagues) offers free decodable books, an example of an open decodable resource. — [UNC TRI Decodable Books](https://tri.fpg.unc.edu/decodable-books/) (not read in detail)
- Little Wandle (Letters and Sounds Revised) provides books matched to its programme, and its Y2 fluency programme is an example of a structured repeated-reading model. — [Little Wandle FAQ: matched decodable books](https://faqs.littlewandlelettersandsounds.org.uk/knowledge/does-little-wandle-have-matched-decodable-books); [Little Wandle Fluency Programme for Y2 (school copy)](https://files.schudio.com/high-green-primary-school/files/documents/Little_Wandle_Fluency_Programme_for_Y2..pdf) (not read in detail; the "3 reads: decoding, prosody, comprehension" model is widely described but **not verified here**)

### Inferences (design recommendations)
- **A decodability engine as the core tool:** keep a machine-readable phonics sequence (GPC units 1..N plus a tricky-word list per unit). For each word, store its grapheme segmentation (e.g., `sh|i|p`, `c|a|k-e`). A word is decodable at unit *k* if all its GPCs are taught by *k*. A story is publishable at unit *k* if at least about 95% of tokens are decodable or taught tricky words (CONVENTION; the commonly used thresholds range from about 80% to 100%, and no primary evidence for a specific cutoff was found). Aim for 100% in the first units, where the child has the fewest strategies.
- **Other text constraints by stage (CONVENTION):** early units use sentences of 3–6 words, 1–2 sentences per page, a picture that supports but does not give away the words, and no more than about 1–2 new tricky words per story, pre-taught before reading. Later units allow longer sentences and multisyllabic words built from taught syllable types.
- **Comprehension objectives:** pair each decodable story with 1–3 questions (literal, then one inference), answered by tapping pictures. This follows Cheatham & Allor's warning that decodability alone is insufficient, and suits EFL children who need meaning support. Attach a picture glossary or Japanese gloss on tap for vocabulary unknown to EFL learners, so decoding success is not confused with a vocabulary gap.
- **Repeated reading flow (CONVENTION, from the UK practice of reading each book about 3 times):** Read 1 decodes with word-tap support (sound-button highlighting). Read 2 builds fluency (child reads, model audio available, optional echo reading). Read 3 checks comprehension and expression.
- **Transition rule:** move to less controlled texts once the child has mastered the basic code (single letters, digraphs, common vowel teams) and decodes unseen pseudo-words fluently. Still filter texts by the percentage of unknown GPCs.
- **Generating stories with an LLM:** constrain generation to the unit's allowed-word list, then **validate automatically** with the decodability engine (reject or rewrite any token not decodable at unit *k*). Have a human review naturalness and age-appropriateness. Never ship unvalidated generated text.

### Gaps
- Primary studies not retrieved: Juel & Roper-Schneider (1985), Mesmer (2001/2005), Price-Mohr & Price (2020), and any 2020s RCTs on the decodable-to-authentic transition.
- The primary DfE Reading Framework (2023, revised 2025?) wording on "fully decodable" and "matched to the programme's sequence" was not fetched.
- I found no evidence-based decodability percentage threshold. The 80/90/95/100% figures circulating are conventions.
- UFLI Foundations decodable passages, Open Court, and open "decodable text checker" tools were not examined; their licences and terms need checking before reuse.
- No research found on decodable texts specifically for Japanese EFL children or other EFL children (vocabulary knowledge confounds decodability).
